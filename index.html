Building robot policies that hold up in the real world.

I'm an undergraduate researcher working on learning-based robot manipulation. I'm interested in what it actually takes to move a policy out of simulation and into the real world, where demonstrations are scarce, tasks unfold over many stages, and small errors compound.

Over the summer I was a Research Intern at the Field Robotics Laboratory, ETRI, where I fine-tuned a vision-language-action model on simulated demonstrations and deployed it on a real robot arm. I also lead a capstone project on imitation learning for long-horizon dual-arm manipulation, supported by the Hanium ICT Mentoring Program.

I'm completing my BS in Intelligent Robotics Engineering at Seoul National University of Science and Technology (expected Feb 2027, GPA 3.92/4.5).

My goal is to build manipulation policies that hold up outside the lab: learning efficiently from limited data, recovering from their own failures, and staying reliable across the long sequences of steps that real tasks demand.

Sim-to-Real Fine-tuning of Vision-Language-Action Models

I looked at whether a vision-language-action policy trained only on simulated demonstrations can run on real hardware. I collected demonstrations in Isaac Sim through leader-follower teleoperation, then worked hands-on with vision-language-action models — training and running inference myself to deploy a fine-tuned π0 policy on a physical SO-101 arm picking up a marker and dropping it into a cup.

Success in simulation climbed to 80% as I added demonstrations and tuned how far ahead the policy predicts, but the same policy reached only about 30% on the real robot. Mixing real-world demonstrations into training did not close that gap, which pointed at the coverage of the data and the absence of recovery behaviour rather than sheer volume.

I also trained GR00T N1.5 under the same conditions to see how its separated vision-language and action modules compare with π0's single unified model.

Beyond this, I experimented with starVLA and MimicGen for robot action data augmentation, and explored reinforcement-learning-based fine-tuning as an alternative approach.

Diffusion Policy, Imitation Learning, Dual-arm manipulation, YOLO

Imitation Learning for Long-Horizon Manipulation

A dual-arm robot picks up an item, scans its barcode, and sets it down. The task only counts as finished if several stages happen in the right order, and that turned out to be where the difficulty lives. A Diffusion Policy trained on demonstrations learned to scan correctly, then went back and scanned again, and kept doing so indefinitely.

The camera views on the way to the scanner and on the way back are nearly identical, so the policy had no way to tell which half of the task it was in. I'm now conditioning the policy on an explicit stage signal, so that states which look the same but call for opposite actions can be told apart.

Qwen, Vision-Language Models, Jetson Thor, vLLM

Vision-Language Models as Success Detectors for Data Collection

A policy trained on a few dozen demonstrations succeeds sometimes and fails often, and someone has to sort the resulting episodes before they can be reused for training. I built a pipeline that hands that judgment to a vision-language model instead. A quantized Qwen model runs on a Jetson Thor next to the robot and labels each rollout as success, failure, or too ambiguous to call, keeping only the episodes worth learning from.

How the question is posed matters more than I expected. Giving the model a reference image of the finished task, rather than describing success in words, made its verdicts markedly more reliable. The model can also state why an episode failed, which turns rejected data into something diagnostic rather than discarded.
